Wave optics · partially coherent sunlight

Why a blurry spiderweb turns into a barcode

Each tiny glue droplet on a sticky thread sends back a pinpoint of sunlight. Out of focus, every pinpoint becomes a disc. Where neighboring discs overlap, their light interferes, because sunlight is still coherent over a few droplet spacings. The simulator below computes that interference directly.

Questions so far

Questions from Dick and Mitra

Why can one striped band run so far along a thread?

Each short piece of the band comes from the few drops right next to it, and the next piece from the next few drops. Coherence only limits how far apart two neighboring drops can be and still interfere, about 70 µm. It doesn't limit how long the band is. The stripes start where the blur has grown big enough for neighboring discs to overlap, and they continue for as long as the thread stays out of focus.

Why do some bands look pale or nearly white?

Drops spaced near or beyond the coherence width make only faint stripes, so the overlapping light looks mostly white. Overexposure also pushes colors toward white, so a darker exposure of the same band is a useful check.

Why are there no colors in the shade?

In shade, the light comes from a big patch of sky instead of the small sun. The coherence width shrinks to a few microns, so neighboring drops can no longer interfere. The blurry band stays, but the stripes disappear.

Dragline silk has no glue drops. Why does it show stripes?

Any variation in brightness along a thread acts like a row of tiny light sources. A dragline is usually two strands laid side by side that touch, separate and twist, and its surface has bumps and fibers. Bumps within about 70 µm of each other interfere and make the same kind of barcode, with a more random pattern. The coherence width also caps the number of stripes across the band at about 2·NA·d₀/λ, whatever the bumps are like.

Does the light go through the drop or around its edge?

Both happen. Zawischa considered light bending around the edge the main source, and so does Dick. For the stripes it makes no difference. Each drop only needs to send out a small, steady point of light, and that is all the simulator assumes.

What does coherence actually mean here?

Demo 2 below lets you build it up. Set "Points on the sun" to 1 and you get one perfect stripe pattern. Add points one at a time and watch each new point's pattern land slightly shifted. Close drops keep the stripes; far-apart drops wash them out.

What the photos show

Dick's own caption for the photo of a single thread was the key clue: "the transition from ball to interference as the images overlap." A lone droplet gives a plain disc. The stripes only show up where the blurred discs of neighboring droplets overlap. That points to interference between droplets, not inside one droplet.

Three ideas explain the stripes. Each one has its own demo below: what a single droplet does to light, why sunlight can interfere at all, and how two droplets make stripes.

Demo 1 · One droplet

What one droplet does to light

Light comes in from the left as a flat wave. The dots mark wave crests, one wavelength apart, drawn to true scale for a 12 µm droplet. Light slows down inside the droplet, so the crests bunch up and the wavefront bends. The light that passes through comes to a rough focus just behind the drop. Seen from far away, that focus is a single bright pinpoint, the glint.

A droplet does not scramble the light. The same wave in always gives the same wave out, with every crest in a fixed place. Two droplets lit by the same wave therefore give two glints whose crests stay in step. Any loss of coherence has to come from the light that arrives, not from the droplets.

Light also bends around the drop's edge (diffraction). Zawischa considered that the main source, and so does Dick. For the stripes it makes no difference: either way, each drop sends out a small, steady point of light.

Water is 1.33. Spider glue is around 1.4 to 1.5.

Line brightness follows the Fresnel equations. Only about 2 to 4% of the light reflects off the surface, and the rainbow path carries a few percent more. Dick's idea is that those two paths interfere with each other. They do, and that is how glories and supernumerary rainbows form. But a 12 µm drop's own fringes are about 0.05 rad wide, so only one or two fit across a camera lens. They also show up in a lone drop's disc, which the photos don't show.

Demo 2 · Coherence

Why sunlight interferes, but only over short distances

Interference needs the light at two places to keep a steady timing relationship, so that crests from one keep landing on crests or troughs from the other. That steadiness is called coherence.

Every point on the sun shines on its own, out of step with every other point. Light from one point on the sun arrives as a flat wave, tilted slightly by where that point sits on the disc. A single tilted wave hitting two droplets gives a perfectly steady fringe pattern, shifted sideways by an amount that depends on the tilt and on how far apart the droplets are.

The camera adds up the patterns from every point on the sun. When the droplets are close, all the patterns line up and the fringes survive. When the droplets are far apart, the patterns land in different places and blur into flat light. Each colored curve below is one point on the sun. The white curve is what the camera records.

Start at 1 and add points. They fill in from the middle of the sun outward.
Sun 4.65 mrad. Zero is a point source, like a star or a laser. Shade is much larger.
coherence(d) = 2·J₁(u) / u, u = 2π·α·d / λ
first zero at d₀ = 0.61 λ / α ≈ 72 µm for the sun at 550 nm

This is the van Cittert–Zernike theorem. Michelson used the same effect in 1920 to measure the size of Betelgeuse: a star's light stays coherent over meters, and the distance at which its fringes vanish gives its angular size. A second kind of coherence matters too. Sunlight mixes all colors, and each color has its own fringe spacing, so only the first few fringes stay sharp before the colors drift apart. That color drift is what makes the bars colored instead of gray.

Demo 3 · Two glints, one sensor

How overlapping blur discs make stripes

Here the camera lens is on the left and the light runs to the right. Each glint's light leaves the lens as a cone that narrows to a sharp image point at the focus. The sensor sits in front of the focus, so each glint lands as a blurry disc. Where two cones overlap, their waves cross at a slight angle. Crests meet crests along some lines and troughs along others, which makes straight bright and dark bands. Drag the sensor line to see the pattern it records.

Turn coherence down and the two waves drift in and out of step. The bands then wander, and the time-averaged strip at the bottom fades to flat. Switch to white light and the bands split into colors, because red light has wider bands than blue.

You can also drag the dashed line.

The wavelength and angles are greatly enlarged so the waves are visible. The real geometry has the same structure: the number of bands across one disc is about 2·NA·d/λ, whatever the defocus.

Putting it together

Each droplet on a capture thread is 10 to 15 µm across. Light that passes through it, or bends around its edge, leaves it as a small, steady pinpoint. With the camera focused somewhere else, each pinpoint spreads into a disc the shape of the lens opening. Neighboring droplets closer together than the sun's coherence width, about 70 µm, make Young's fringes where their discs overlap. Dew drops sit hundreds of microns apart, far outside that width. That is why the sticky threads stripe and the dew-laden ones don't.

The stripes run across the thread because each pair of droplets makes straight fringes perpendicular to the line joining them. Every droplet lies on the same line, so all the fringe sets line up. How many fringes fit across one blur disc depends only on the lens and the droplet spacing d:

fringes per disc ≈ 2·NA·d / λ

With f/2.8 at 15 cm and 30 µm spacing that is about five, which matches the photos. The spacing is irregular, so the fringe sets from different pairs add up to an irregular barcode. Each color has its own fringe period, so the bars come out colored. This is one-dimensional speckle in white light.

Defocus does not change the number of stripes per disc. It changes the disc size, so more defocus gives wider bands with wider stripes. Stopping the lens down gives fewer, wider stripes.

Dick's magnifier observation fits the same picture. Looking through a 10× loupe focused just off the thread, you see the thread and, a short distance away, the colored band. That distance is the blur-disc radius, and the band runs all the way along the thread because every droplet has neighbors.

Where this could be wrong

The explanation depends on droplet spacing. Zawischa counted 12 to 13 droplets per millimeter on a garden spider's thread, about 80 µm apart. That is just past the sun's first coherence zero, where the theory predicts stripes with only about 10% contrast. Inoue and colleagues saw spacings near 0.1 mm on a joro spider and 10 to 20 µm on other species. Measuring the spacing on the threads in these photos is the most useful next step.

A single droplet can also make more than one glint. Zawischa describes extra highlights at the droplet's "neck" where it meets the thread. Those glints sit on the thread's axis a few microns apart, so they would also make stripes across the thread, though fewer and wider ones.

Quantum effects?

None are needed. Classical wave optics plus the partial coherence of sunlight accounts for everything here. A quantum treatment gives the same answer, because each photon interferes only with itself, and partial coherence is the averaging over where on the sun it came from.

Tests that would settle it

  • Use a laser pointer or a pinhole-filtered light. The light becomes fully coherent, so stripes should appear even between widely spaced dew drops.
  • Change the aperture. Stopping down from f/2.8 to f/8 should cut the stripes per disc to about a third.
  • Photograph the same thread in focus, measure the droplet spacing, and compare the stripe count against 2·NA·d/λ.
  • Shoot under thin cloud or a larger light source. The coherence width shrinks and the stripes should fade, while the bokeh band stays.

Other research

  • Dietrich Zawischa, “Optische Erscheinungen an Spinnennetzen” (in German). This is the closest match. Zawischa attributes the colors to interference of light from the nearly evenly spaced glue droplets. He notes they show best slightly out of focus, because they form where light from neighboring droplets overlaps, and he rules out rainbows for these colors. He doesn't discuss the coherence of sunlight.
  • Inoue, Imamura & Sato, “Why does the spider silk shine with rainbow colors?” (Bulletin of NIT Tsuyama College 61, 2019, in Japanese, PDF). They treat each droplet as a light source and compute the interference, and they photographed defocused threads with colors spread across the thread. Their model predicts too much blue compared with the pink and green actually seen, which they leave open.
  • Kane, Staib, Naidoo, Little & Herberstein, “Optics of spider ‘sticky’ orb webs” (Proc. SPIE 7975, 2011). This is the opposing view. From white-light and laser experiments they conclude the main color source is rainbows from the droplets, explained by geometric optics. This is closest to Dick's idea. A follow-up, “Micro-optical elements and optical materials of certain spider webs” (SPIE 8428, 2012), describes the glue droplets as near-elliptical micro-lenses.
  • Atmospheric Optics, “Spider Silk”. This short note says out-of-focus colors on capture threads come at least partly from scattering by the arrays of globules, and colors on dry threads come from diffraction.
  • Illinois Physics Van, “Colored Spider Webs”. A photographer's question, answered by pointing to Zawischa's droplet-interference explanation.

What the big simulator does

Droplets are point scatterers at quasi-random positions along a line. Each one produces a defocused spherical wave clipped to the blur disc. For each of 16 wavelengths from 400 to 700 nm, the field from each droplet pair is multiplied by the sun's mutual coherence 2J₁(kαd)/(kαd) and summed. The result is converted to color with the CIE 1931 color-matching functions. With "Thread slants through focus", each drop gets its own blur radius, and brightness is evened out along the band for display. "Surface roughness" gives each scatterer a random height, which shifts its phase by 2kh. The simulator draws every blur disc as evenly lit, while real lenses give discs with bright rims or rings, like those in Dick's photos. It also leaves out Mie scattering from each droplet, the viewing angle relative to the sun, and the thread itself.
